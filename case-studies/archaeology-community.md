# Samples in the archaeology community

## About the archaeology community

The archaeology community investigates all facets of past human lives that manifest in materials without clear definition of when the "past" starts. Archaeology has many subdisciplines. For example, this case study is reported by a person from the archaeological sciences, which are dedicated to the investigation of archaeological remains with scientific methods. It is a highly interdisciplinary community, with overlaps in scope and members into e.g. geosciences, art history, numismatics, conservation \& restoration.

## Samples in the archaeology community

Because all materials and objects that humans created, modified or interacted with can be considered object for archaeological research, the range of materials is huge and increases with time (for example, plastics and rubber are relatively young). The range grows, if fields such as environmental archaeology are considered, i.e. how humans interacted with and shaped the environment (e.g. by deforestation evidenced by in pollen profiles).

Over the history of mankind, the most common material types were probably:

* Geological materials, esp. stones
* Biogenic materials, esp. wood, bones and antler
* Pottery
* Metals and metallurgical by-products (e.g. slag)
* Glass
* Soil

They are turned into samples/specimens depending on the method used to investigate. The most common ones created by the reporting person are:

* Fragments and complete objects (as found on excavations)
* Cuts/fragments
* (Thin) sections
* Powders/drillings

Many more exist and come in all shapes in sizes, from entire statues to molecular tracers (e.g. aDNA, proteins). 

## Characterising properties

Given the range, they can be differentiated within the domain by:

* Location
* Dating
* Material properties (e.g., anthropogenic/biogenic/geogenic, metal/glass/pottery, ...)
* Object type (e.g., vessel/coin/axe/bone)

The distinguishing characteristic feature from other domains is probably only their age and their relationship to human activity. However, when it comes to e.g. experimental archaeology, materials and samples are recent but produced in a more or less authentic reproduction of ancient processes. The relationship to human activity can be very faint and indirect, as indicated above.
